Chapter 6: Correlation and Regression

Section 6.2: Correlation

In many applications, two variables are measured on the same individual or object. For example, we may record a person's height and weight, a student's hours of study and exam score, or the outside temperature and electricity usage. In these situations, we are interested in describing and measuring the relationship between the variables.

Positive and Negative Correlation

Definition: Correlation

Correlation is a numerical measure of the strength and direction of association between two quantitative variables. In this chapter, we focus on linear correlation, which measures how closely the paired data follow a straight-line pattern.

Definition: Positive Linear Correlation

A positive linear correlation occurs when larger values of one variable tend to be associated with larger values of the other variable.

For example, taller people generally weigh more than shorter people.

Definition: Negative Linear Correlation

A negative linear correlation occurs when larger values of one variable tend to be associated with smaller values of the other variable.

For example, as the outside temperature increases, home heating costs generally decrease.

If there is no apparent straight-line relationship between the variables, the data are said to have little or no linear correlation.

Scatterplots are often used to visualize these relationships before computing a numerical measure of correlation.

Always examine a scatterplot before interpreting a correlation coefficient.

Pearson Correlation Coefficient

Definition: Pearson Correlation Coefficient

The most commonly used measure of linear correlation is the Pearson correlation coefficient, denoted by \(\rho\) for a population and \(r\) for a sample.

For a sample consisting of paired observations

\[(x_1,y_1),\,(x_2,y_2),\,\ldots,\,(x_n,y_n),\]

the sample Pearson correlation coefficient is

\[r=\frac{n\sum x_iy_i-\left(\sum x_i\right)\left(\sum y_i\right)}{\sqrt{\left[n\sum x_i^2-\left(\sum x_i\right)^2\right]\left[n\sum y_i^2-\left(\sum y_i\right)^2\right]}}.\]

The Pearson correlation coefficient is defined only when both variables have nonzero variation.

The Pearson correlation coefficient always satisfies

\[-1\le r\le1.\]

The correlation coefficient \(r\) measures the direction and strength of a linear relationship. While values of \(r\) closer to \(\pm1\) indicate stronger linear relationships and values closer to 0 indicate weaker relationships, there are no universally accepted cutoffs for classifying a correlation as weak, moderate, or strong. The interpretation depends on the field of study, the quality of the data, and the purpose of the analysis. As a result, any classification of strength should be viewed as a guideline rather than a strict rule.

The following ranges are one possible set of informal guidelines. Different fields and textbooks use different conventions. These labels should not be treated as mathematical definitions.

Informal guidelines for interpreting \(r\)
Value of \(r\)Interpretation
\(r=1\)Perfect positive linear correlation
\(0.8<r<1\)Strong positive correlation
\(0.5<r\le0.8\)Moderate positive correlation
\(0.2<r\le0.5\)Weak positive correlation
\(-0.2\le r\le 0.2\)Little or no linear correlation
\(-0.5\le r<-0.2\)Weak negative correlation

Coefficient of Determination

Definition: Coefficient of Determination

The square of the Pearson correlation coefficient, \(r^2\), is called the coefficient of determination.

The coefficient of determination represents the proportion of the variation in the response variable that is explained by the linear relationship with the explanatory variable.

For example, \(r=0.90\) gives \(r^2=(0.90)^2=0.81\). Thus, approximately \(81\%\) of the variation in the response variable is explained by the linear model. The remaining \(19\%\) is variation not accounted for by the model.

The column totals are \(\sum x=15\), \(\sum y=330\), \(\sum x^2=55\), \(\sum y^2=22150\), and \(\sum xy=1050\). Since \(n=5\), the correlation coefficient is

\[r=\frac{5(1050)-15(330)}{\sqrt{[5(55)-15^2][5(22150)-330^2]}}=\frac{300}{\sqrt{50(1850)}}\approx0.986.\]

The correlation coefficient is very close to 1, indicating a very strong positive linear relationship between hours studied and exam score.

The coefficient of determination is

\[r^2=(0.986)^2\approx0.972.\]

Thus, approximately \(97.2\%\) of the variation in exam scores is accounted for by the fitted linear regression model using hours studied and \(2.8\%\) is not accounted for by the model.

Important Notes

Correlation measures the strength of a linear relationship. Two variables may have a strong nonlinear relationship while having a correlation coefficient close to zero.

Furthermore, a correlation between two variables indicates an association, but correlation alone does not establish that changes in one variable cause changes in the other.

Key Points

  • Correlation measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two variables.
  • The Pearson correlation coefficient ranges from \(-1\) to \(1\).
  • Positive values indicate positive linear correlation; negative values indicate negative linear correlation.
  • Values near \(\pm1\) indicate strong linear relationships.
  • The coefficient of determination is \(r^2\).
  • The value of \(r^2\) represents the proportion of the variation explained by the linear model.
  • Correlation measures association, not causation.
