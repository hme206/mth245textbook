Appendix D: Glossary

A B C D E F H I L M N O P Q R S T U V W

A

Addition Rule
A rule used to find the probability that at least one of two events occurs: \[P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B).\]
Alternative Hypothesis
A statement that contradicts the null hypothesis and represents the effect or difference being tested.

B

Bar Chart
A graph that uses separate bars to display the frequencies or percentages of categorical data.
Binomial Distribution
The probability distribution for the number of successes in a fixed number of independent binomial trials.
Binomial Experiment
A probability experiment with a fixed number of independent trials, two possible outcomes per trial, and a constant probability of success.
Box-and-Whisker Plot
A graph that displays the five-number summary: minimum, first quartile, median, third quartile, and maximum.

C

Categorical Data
Data consisting of names, labels, or categories rather than numerical measurements.
Census
A study that collects information from every member of a population.
Central Limit Theorem
A theorem stating that, under appropriate conditions, the sampling distribution of the sample mean becomes approximately normal as sample size increases.
Circle Graph
A circular graph, also called a pie chart, that displays categories as sections of a whole.
Coefficient of Determination
The value \(r^2\), which represents the proportion of variation in a response variable explained by a linear regression model.
Coefficient of Variation
The standard deviation divided by the mean, often expressed as a percentage; it measures relative variability.
Combinations
Selections of objects where the order of the objects does not matter.
Conditional Probability
The probability that an event occurs given that another event has already occurred: \[P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.\]
Confidence Bound
An endpoint of a confidence interval used to estimate a population parameter.
Confidence Interval
A range of plausible values for a population parameter, calculated from sample data at a specified confidence level.
Continuous Random Variable
A random variable that can take any value within an interval, such as height or temperature.
Continuous Variable
A variable that can take any value within a range, including decimal values.
Cumulative Frequency
The total frequency of observations up to and including a particular value or class.
Cumulative Relative Frequency
The proportion or percentage of observations at or below a particular value or class.

D

Decile
A value that divides an ordered data set into ten equal parts.
Dependent Events
Events for which the occurrence of one event changes the probability of the other.
Descriptive Statistics
Methods used to organize, summarize, and display data.
Discrete Random Variable
A random variable that can take a countable number of distinct values.
Discrete Variable
A variable that can take separate, countable values, usually whole numbers.

E

Empirical Rule
For a normal distribution, approximately \(68\%\) of data falls within \(1\) standard deviation of the mean, \(95\%\) falls within \(2\) standard deviations, and \(99.7\%\) falls within \(3\) standard deviations: \[68\%\approx\mu\pm\sigma,\qquad 95\%\approx\mu\pm2\sigma,\qquad 99.7\%\approx\mu\pm3\sigma.\]
Equally Likely Outcomes
Outcomes that have the same probability of occurring.
Event
A set of one or more outcomes from a sample space.
Expected Value
The long-run average value of a random variable: \[E(X)=\sum xP(x).\]
Extrapolation
A prediction made outside the range of observed predictor values.

F

Factorial Notation
The notation \(n!\), meaning: \[n!=n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(2)(1).\]
Frequency Distribution
A table or display showing how often each value or class occurs.
Fundamental Counting Principle
A rule stating that if one task can be done in \(m\) ways and a second task in \(n\) ways, both tasks can be done in \(mn\) ways.

H

Histogram
A graph that uses touching bars to display the distribution of numerical data grouped into intervals.
Hypothesis Test
A statistical procedure used to evaluate a claim about a population parameter.

I

Independent Events
Events where the occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other.
Inferential Statistics
Methods used to draw conclusions about a population based on sample data.
Interpolation
A prediction made within the range of observed predictor values.
Intersection
The set of outcomes common to two or more events, written \(A\cap B\).
Interval Measure
A measurement scale with equal intervals between values but no meaningful zero point.

L

Linear Regression
A statistical method used to model and predict the relationship between two quantitative variables using a line.

M

Mean
The arithmetic average of a data set: \[\bar{x}=\frac{\sum x}{n}.\]
Median
The middle value of an ordered data set; if there are two middle values, their average is used.
Modality
The number and pattern of peaks or modes in a distribution, such as unimodal or bimodal.
Mode
The value or category that occurs most frequently in a data set.
Multiplication Rule
A rule used to find the probability that two events both occur: \[P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A).\]
Mutually Exclusive Events
Events that cannot occur at the same time; their intersection is empty.

N

Nominal Measure
A measurement scale consisting of categories with no inherent order.
Normal Distribution
A symmetric, bell-shaped probability distribution characterized by its mean and standard deviation.
Null Hypothesis
The hypothesis that states there is no effect, difference, or relationship in the population being studied.
Numerical Data
Data represented by numbers that describe counts or measurements.

O

Ogive
A graph that shows the cumulative frequency of data constructed by plotting cumulative frequencies against the corresponding class boundaries and connecting the points with line segments. Also called a cumulative frequency graph.
Ordinal Measure
A measurement scale with categories that have a meaningful order, but differences between categories are not necessarily equal.
Outcome
A single possible result of a random experiment.
Outliers
Observations that are unusually far from the rest of the data.

P

Parameter
A numerical value that describes a characteristic of an entire population.
Pascal's Triangle
A triangular arrangement of numbers in which each interior number is the sum of the two numbers directly above it. It can be used to find binomial coefficients.
Pearson Correlation Coefficient
A number \(r\) between \(-1\) and \(1\) that measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two quantitative variables.
Percentile
A value below which a specified percentage of observations in a data set falls.
Permutations
Arrangements of objects where the order of the objects matters.
Population
The complete group of individuals or objects of interest in a statistical study.
Power
The probability that a hypothesis test correctly rejects a false null hypothesis.
Probability
A numerical measure from 0 to 1 that describes how likely an event is to occur.
Probability Density Function
A function that describes the relative likelihood of values for a continuous random variable. Probabilities are found from areas under the curve.
Probability Distribution
A description of the possible values of a random variable and their associated probabilities.
Probability Mass Function
A function that gives the probability of each possible value of a discrete random variable.
P-value
The probability, assuming the null hypothesis is true, of obtaining a result at least as extreme as the observed result.

Q

Qualitative Variable
A variable whose values are categories or qualities rather than numerical measurements.
Quantile
A value that divides an ordered data set into specified proportions.
Quantitative Variable
A variable whose values are numerical measurements or counts.

R

Random Experiment
A process that produces one outcome from a known set of possible outcomes, with the exact outcome uncertain beforehand.
Random Variable
A variable whose numerical value is determined by the outcome of a random experiment.
Range
The difference between the maximum and minimum values in a data set: \[\text{Range}=\text{Maximum}-\text{Minimum}.\]
Range Rule of Thumb
An approximate rule stating that the standard deviation can be estimated by dividing the range by 4: \[s\approx\frac{\text{Range}}{4}.\]
Ratio Measure
A measurement scale with equal intervals and a meaningful zero, allowing meaningful ratios between values.
Relative Frequency
The frequency of an observation or category divided by the total number of observations.

S

Sample
A subset of a population selected for a statistical study.
Sample Space
The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment.
Skewness
The degree to which a distribution is asymmetrical.
Standard Deviation
A measure of how spread out data values are from their mean.
Standard Normal Distribution
A normal distribution with mean \(0\) and standard deviation \(1\).
Statistic
A numerical value that describes a characteristic of a sample.
Statistics
The science of collecting, organizing, analyzing, interpreting, and presenting data.
Stem-and-Leaf Plot
A display that separates data values into stems and leaves while preserving the original values.
Student's t Distribution
A family of probability distributions used especially for inference about a population mean when the population standard deviation is unknown.
Survey
A method of collecting information from individuals, usually by asking questions.

T

Trimmed Mean
A mean calculated after removing a specified percentage of the smallest and largest observations.
Type I Error
The error of rejecting a true null hypothesis; a false positive.
Type II Error
The error of failing to reject a false null hypothesis; a false negative.

U

Union
The set of outcomes belonging to at least one of two events, written \(A\cup B\).
Universal Set
The set containing all objects or outcomes being considered in a particular situation.

V

Variance
A measure of variability equal to the average squared distance of observations from their mean: \[\sigma^2=\frac{\sum(x-\mu)^2}{N}.\]
Venn Diagram
A diagram that uses overlapping circles or other shapes to represent sets and their relationships.

W

Weighted Mean
A mean in which different values are given different levels of importance, or weights: \[\bar{x}_w=\frac{\sum wx}{\sum w}.\]
