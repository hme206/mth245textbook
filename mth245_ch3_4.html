Section 3.4: Experimental Data

Observational Studies and Experiments

There are two primary methods of conducting statistical studies.

Definition: Observational Study

An observational study is one in which researchers observe subjects and record information without attempting to influence the responses.

Observational studies are useful when it is unethical, impractical, or impossible to impose treatments.

Examples include:

  • Studying the relationship between smoking and lung disease.
  • Recording the daily temperatures in a city.
  • Observing wildlife in a national park.

Observational studies can identify associations between variables but generally cannot establish cause-and-effect relationships.

Definition: Experiment

An experiment is a study in which researchers deliberately impose one or more treatments on subjects and observe the responses.

Experiments are designed to provide evidence about whether a treatment causes a change in a response variable.

Examples include:

  • Comparing two blood pressure medications.
  • Testing the effectiveness of a new fertilizer.
  • Evaluating a new teaching method.

Well-designed experiments allow researchers to make conclusions about cause-and-effect relationships.

Treatment and Control Groups

Experiments often compare two or more groups.

Definition: Treatment Group

A treatment group, or treatment groups, receives the experimental treatment or condition being studied.

Definition: Control Group

A control group receives no treatment, the current standard treatment, or a placebo and serves as a basis for comparison.

Comparing the responses of the treatment and control groups helps researchers compare the responses associated with the different conditions.

Confounding Variables

Definition: Confounding Variable

A confounding variable is a variable whose effects cannot be separated from the effect of the treatment or explanatory variable, making it difficult to determine the treatment's effect on the response.

For example, suppose researchers compare two exercise programs but one group is considerably younger than the other. Age may influence the results, making it difficult to determine whether differences are due to the exercise programs or to age.

Careful experimental design attempts to minimize the effects of confounding variables.

Randomization

Random assignment of subjects to treatment groups helps produce comparable groups and reduces the influence of confounding variables.

Randomization does not eliminate variability or guarantee identical treatment groups. Instead, it tends to balance known and unknown characteristics across treatment groups, reducing the risk that systematic differences between groups will confound the treatment comparison.

Definition: Completely Randomized Design

A completely randomized design randomly assigns all experimental subjects to the available treatment groups.

Example: One hundred patients are randomly assigned to receive either a new medication or the current medication.

Sometimes researchers recognize that subjects differ in important ways before the experiment begins.

Definition: Block

A block is a group of experimental subjects that are similar with respect to a characteristic expected to affect the response variable.

Common blocking variables include:

  • Age
  • Gender
  • Geographic region
  • Educational level

Definition: Randomized Block Design

A randomized block design divides subjects into homogeneous blocks and then randomly assigns treatments within each block.

Blocking reduces variation caused by known differences among subjects, allowing a more precise comparison of treatments.

Blocking is not the same as random assignment. Subjects are first grouped into blocks and then randomly assigned to treatments within each block.

Example: A researcher studying two teaching methods divides students into blocks based on grade level. Within each grade level, students are randomly assigned to one of the two teaching methods.

Unintended Sources of Bias

Definition: Placebo Effect

The placebo effect refers to changes in a participant's response that may occur because the participant expects to receive an effective treatment, rather than because of the treatment's active ingredient.

Definition: Placebo

A placebo is an inactive treatment designed to resemble the actual treatment.

For example, participants receiving a sugar pill may report reduced symptoms simply because they believe they are receiving medication.

Definition: Hawthorne Effect

The Hawthorne effect occurs when individuals modify their behavior because they know they are being observed. The term is commonly used to describe changes in behavior associated with awareness of being observed or participating in a study.

For example, workers may temporarily increase productivity simply because they know they are participating in a study.

Definition: Rosenthal Effect

The Rosenthal effect, also called the experimenter expectancy effect, occurs when researchers unintentionally influence the behavior or responses of participants because of their expectations.

For example, an instructor who believes certain students will perform well may unknowingly provide them with additional encouragement.

Blinding

Blinding helps reduce bias during an experiment.

Definition: Single-Blind Experiment

In a single-blind experiment, participants do not know which treatment or experimental condition they receive.

Single blinding helps reduce the placebo effect.

Definition: Double-Blind Experiment

A double-blind experiment is one in which neither the participants nor the researchers directly interacting with the participants know who is receiving the treatment.

Double-blind designs can reduce both participant expectancy effects and researcher expectancy effects and are commonly used in clinical research when practical.

Characteristics of a Good Experiment

Well-designed experiments typically include the following principles:

  1. Control — Keep conditions other than the treatment as consistent as practical.
  2. Randomization — Use random assignment to treatment groups to reduce systematic differences between groups.
  3. Replication — Apply each treatment to multiple experimental units so that random variation can be assessed and treatment effects can be estimated more reliably. Increasing the number of experimental units generally reduces the influence of random variation on estimated treatment differences.
  4. Blinding — When appropriate, conceal treatment assignments from participants and/or researchers to reduce expectancy-related bias.

Following these principles improves the reliability and validity of experimental results.
