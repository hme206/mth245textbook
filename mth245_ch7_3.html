Section 7.3: Standard Normal Distribution

The Standard Normal Distribution

Normal distributions can have many different combinations of means and standard deviations. For example, the distributions \(N(50,4)\) and \(N(50,12)\) are both normal distributions centered at 50, but they have different amounts of spread, while \(N(70,4)\) is shifted considerably to the right.

The Normal Density Function

The generic formula for the normal distribution is

\[f(x;\mu,\sigma)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}},\]

where

\[x=\text{individual observation},\] \[\mu=\text{population mean},\]

and

\[\sigma=\text{population standard deviation}.\]

As you can imagine, trying to work with this formula becomes cumbersome. To make comparisons between different normal distributions easier, we use a special normal distribution called the standard normal distribution.

Definition: Standard Normal Distribution

The standard normal distribution is a normal distribution with

\[\mu=0,\qquad \sigma=1,\qquad \text{therefore }\sigma^2=1\]

and is denoted by

\[Z\sim N(0,1^2).\]

The standard normal distribution is centered at zero and measured in units of standard deviations. The horizontal axis is called the standard score or z-score.

Standardizing a Normal Variable

Any normally distributed variable can be converted into a standard normal variable by measuring how many standard deviations an observation is above or below the mean.

Definition: z-Score

The z-score is defined as

\[z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma},\]

where

\[x=\text{individual observation},\] \[\mu=\text{population mean},\]

and

\[\sigma=\text{population standard deviation}.\]

A positive z-score indicates that the observation is above the mean, while a negative z-score indicates that the observation is below the mean.

Interpreting the sign of a z-score
z-scoreInterpretation
\(z<0\)Observation is below the mean
\(z=0\)Observation is exactly at the mean
\(z>0\)Observation is above the mean

Example 1

Suppose adult male heights are approximately normally distributed with \(\mu=70\) inches and \(\sigma=3\) inches. Find the z-score for a person who is 76 inches tall.

Solution

Using

\[z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma},\]

we have

\[z=\frac{76-70}{3}\] \[z=\frac{6}{3}=2.\]

A height of 76 inches is therefore 2 standard deviations above the mean.

Using the Standard Normal Distribution

Once a value has been converted to a z-score, probabilities can be found using the standard normal distribution.

The area under the standard normal curve represents probability. Because the total area under the curve is 1,

\[P(-\infty<Z<\infty)=1.\]

Common probabilities are often found using a standard normal table similar to the one found in this text.

The standard normal table is a table used to find probabilities associated with the standard normal distribution: \(Z\sim N(0,1)\). The entries in the table represent the area under the standard normal curve to the left of a given z-score: \(P(Z<z)\).

The table is organized by splitting each z-score into two parts.

  • The row contains the ones and tenths digits of the z-score.
  • The column contains the hundredths digit of the z-score.

Negative z-Scores

\[P(Z<-2.13)\approx0.0166.\]

Because the standard normal distribution is symmetric about zero, probabilities for negative z-scores can also be found using symmetry:

\[P(Z<-a)=1-P(Z<a).\]

For example,

\[P(Z<-2.13)=1-P(Z<2.13),\]

so

\[P(Z<-2.13)=1-0.9834=0.0166.\]

Finding Different Types of Probabilities

The standard normal table can be used to find:

  • the probability that a z-score falls below a value,
  • the probability that a z-score falls above a value,
  • the probability that a z-score falls between two values.

For probabilities below a z-score, \(P(Z<z)\), read the probability directly from the standard normal table.

For probabilities above a z-score, \(P(Z>z)\), use the complement rule:

\[P(Z>z)=1-P(Z<z).\]

For example, to find the probability \(P(Z>2.34)\), read the probability off the table and subtract from 1:

\[1-P(Z<2.34)=1-0.9904=0.0096\]

Verifying the Empirical Rule

We can verify the empirical rule by looking at the values in the standard normal table.

For example, we can look at the area within two standard deviations of the mean. From the table, we can find \(P(Z<2)=0.9772\).

This means approximately 97.72% of standard normal observations are below a z-score of 2.

Similarly,

\[P(-2<Z<2)=0.9772-0.0228=0.9544=95.44\%.\]

This is close to the approximation of \(95\%\).

Looking at values that are a little closer to \(95\%\),

\[P(-1.96<Z<1.96)=0.9750-0.0250=0.9500=95\%.\]

The value \(z=1.96\) is commonly used for a two-sided \(95\%\) confidence interval based on the standard normal distribution.

If we were to look at the area within one standard deviation of the mean,

\[P(-1<Z<1)=0.8413-0.1587=0.6826=68.26\%.\]

This is close to the \(68\%\) value from the empirical rule.

Finally, looking at the area within three standard deviations of the mean yields

\[P(-3<Z<3)=0.9987-0.0013=0.9974=99.74\%.\]

This is close to the \(99.7\%\) value from the empirical rule.

Converting Back to Original Units

The z-score formula can be rearranged to convert a standard score back into an original measurement.

Starting with

\[z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma},\]

multiply both sides by \(\sigma\):

\[z\sigma=x-\mu.\]

Adding \(\mu\) gives

\[x=\mu+z\sigma.\]

This formula allows us to find the original value corresponding to a given percentile or z-score.

Example 2

Exam scores are normally distributed with

\[\mu=80\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma=6.\]

A student has a score with \(z=1.5\). Find the student's exam score.

Solution

Using \(x=\mu+z\sigma\), we get

\[x=80+(1.5)(6)=89.\]

The student's score is 89.

Relationship Between Normal and Standard Normal Distributions

Every normal distribution has the same basic bell-shaped form. The mean determines the center of the distribution, and the standard deviation determines the spread.

The standard normal distribution provides a common scale by converting all normal distributions into the same form.

Normal and standard normal distributions compared
Normal DistributionStandard Normal Distribution
Mean \(\mu\)Mean \(0\)
Standard deviation \(\sigma\)Standard deviation \(1\)
Variable \(X\)Variable \(Z\)
Units of measurementStandard deviations from the mean

Key Points

  • The standard normal distribution is a normal distribution with \(\mu=0,\ \sigma=1.\)
  • Any normal distribution can be converted to a standard normal distribution using \(z=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}.\)
  • A z-score describes the number of standard deviations an observation is from the mean.
  • Positive z-scores indicate values above the mean; negative z-scores indicate values below the mean.
  • The standard normal distribution allows probabilities from different normal distributions to be compared.
  • The original value can be recovered using \(x=\mu+z\sigma.\)
