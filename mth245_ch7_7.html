Chapter 7: Normal Distribution

Section 7.7: Summary

The normal distribution is a symmetric, bell-shaped probability distribution determined by its mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\). Approximately \(68\%\), \(95\%\), and \(99.7\%\) of observations lie within one, two, and three standard deviations of the mean, respectively.

The standard normal distribution has \(\mu=0\) and \(\sigma=1\). Any normal variable can be standardized using

\[z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}.\]

Z-scores measure the number of standard deviations an observation is from the mean and can be used with a standard normal table to find probabilities. The original value can be recovered with

\[x=\mu+z\sigma.\]

The Central Limit Theorem states that the sampling distribution of the sample mean is approximately normal for sufficiently large samples. Its mean and standard error are

\[\mu_{\bar{x}}=\mu,\qquad \sigma_{\bar{x}}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.\]

Thus, larger samples produce less variable sample means.

Finally, a binomial distribution can often be approximated by a normal distribution when

\[np\geq5 \qquad\text{and}\qquad nq\geq5.\]

The approximating distribution has mean \(\mu=np\) and standard deviation \(\sigma=\sqrt{npq}\). A continuity correction of \(0.5\) is used to account for the difference between discrete and continuous distributions.
