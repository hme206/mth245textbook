Binomial Experiments

Many probability experiments consist of a fixed number of identical trials, where each trial results in one of two possible outcomes. Such experiments are called binomial experiments.

Definition: Binomial Experiment

A binomial experiment satisfies the following four conditions:

  1. The experiment consists of a fixed number of trials, \(n\).
  2. Each trial has only two possible outcomes: success and failure.
  3. The trials are independent.
  4. The probability of success, \(p\), remains constant for every trial.

If

\[p=P(\text{success}),\]

then

\[q=P(\text{failure})=1-p.\]

The random variable

\[X=\text{number of successes in }n\text{ trials}\]

is called a binomial random variable.

Examples of Binomial Experiments

The following are examples of binomial experiments.

  • Flipping a coin 20 times and counting the number of heads.
  • Inspecting 15 products and counting the number of defective items.
  • Surveying 100 randomly selected voters from a large population and counting the number who support a proposal.
  • Rolling a die 12 times and counting the number of sixes.

The following are not binomial experiments.

  • Rolling one die and recording the number obtained (more than two outcomes).
  • Drawing cards without replacement (drawing cards without replacement generally does not produce independent trials, so an exact binomial model is not appropriate).
  • Measuring students' heights (continuous variable).
  • Playing games in a post-season tournament (number of trials are not fixed).

Binomial Probability Formula

The Binomial Probability Formula

Suppose a binomial experiment consists of

\[n\]

independent trials with probability of success

\[p.\]

Then the probability of obtaining exactly

\[x\]

successes is

\[P(X=x)=\binom{n}{x}p^xq^{\,n-x},\]

where

\[q=1-p.\]

The quantity

\[\binom{n}{x}\]

counts the number of ways to choose the successful trials. This is the same as the combination \({}_{n}C_{r}\) found in our prior chapter.

The term \(p^xq^{\,n-x}\) gives the probability of one particular sequence containing \(x\) successes and \(n-x\) failures, while \(\binom{n}{x}\) counts the number of such sequences.

Example 1

A fair coin is tossed five times. Find the probability of obtaining exactly three heads.

Solution

Let

\[X=\text{number of heads}.\]

Since

\[n=5,\qquad x=3,\qquad p=0.5,\qquad q=0.5,\]

the binomial probability is

\[P(X=3)=\binom{5}{3}(0.5)^3(0.5)^2=10(0.5)^5=10(0.03125)=0.3125.\]

Therefore,

\[P(X=3)=0.3125.\]

There are \(\binom{n}{x}=10\) possible sequences containing exactly three heads.

Solving Binomial Probability Problems

Binomial problems often ask for probabilities involving several possible values of the random variable.

To find the probability of exactly \(x\) successes,

\[P(X=x),\]

use the binomial formula directly.

“At most” means less than or equal to.

For example,

\[P(X\le3)=P(0)+P(1)+P(2)+P(3).\]

“At least” means greater than or equal to.

For example,

\[P(X\ge4)=P(4)+P(5)+\cdots+P(n).\]

Often it is easier to use the complement:

\[P(X\ge4)=1-P(X\le3).\]

For inclusive values,

\[P(2\le X\le5)=P(2)+P(3)+P(4)+P(5).\]

Example 2

A basketball player makes a free throw with probability \(0.80\). If the player attempts six free throws, find the probability of making exactly five shots.

Solution

\(X\) denotes the number of successful free throws made by the player out of \(n=6\) attempts with the probability of success of each shot \(p=0.80\), and the probability of failure of each shot \(q=0.20\). That gives us

\[X \sim \text{Bin}(6,0.80).\]

We are required to find

\[P(X=5).\]

Applying the binomial probability formula,

\[P(X=5)=\binom{6}{5}(0.80)^5(0.20)=6(0.32768)(0.20)=0.3932.\] \[P(X=5)\approx0.3932.\]

Example 3

A basketball player makes a free throw with probability \(0.80\). If the player attempts six free throws, find the probability of making at least one shot.

Solution

\(X\) still denotes the number of successful free throws made by the player out of \(n=6\) attempts with the probability of success of each shot \(p=0.80\), and the probability of failure of each shot \(q=0.20\). That still gives us

\[X \sim \text{Bin}(6,0.80).\]

We are required to find

\[P(X\ge1).\]

Using the complement rule,

\[P(X\ge1)=1-P(X=0).\]

Applying the binomial probability formula,

\[P(X=x)=\binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{\,n-x},\]

where \(n=6\), \(p=0.80\), and \(x=0\),

\[P(X=0)=\binom{6}{0}(0.80)^0(0.20)^6=1(1)(0.20)^6=0.000064.\]

Therefore,

\[P(X\ge1)=1-P(X=0)=1-0.000064=0.999936.\]

Hence, the probability that the player makes at least one free throw is

\[P(X\ge1)=0.999936\approx0.9999.\]

The complement is especially useful here because “at least one” includes six possible values: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. The complement contains only one value, 0.

Example 4

A basketball player makes a free throw with probability \(0.80\). If the player attempts six free throws, find the probability of making more than four shots.

Solution

\(X\) still denotes the number of successful free throws made by the player out of \(n=6\) attempts with the probability of success of each shot \(p=0.80\), and the probability of failure of each shot \(q=0.20\). That still gives us

\[X \sim \text{Bin}(6,0.80).\]

We are required to find

\[P(X>4)=P(X=5)+P(X=6).\]

Using the binomial probability formula,

\[P(X=x)=\binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{\,n-x},\]

where \(n=6\) and \(p=0.80\),

\[P(X=5)=\binom{6}{5}(0.80)^5(0.20)^1=6(0.32768)(0.20)=0.393216,\]

and

\[P(X=6)=\binom{6}{6}(0.80)^6(0.20)^0=(0.80)^6=0.262144.\]

Therefore,

\[P(X>4)=P(X=5)+P(X=6)=0.393216+0.262144=0.65536.\]

Hence, the probability that the player makes more than four free throws is

\[P(X>4)=0.65536\approx0.6554.\]

Notice that “more than 4” excludes 4, so the possible values are 5 and 6.

Example 5

A basketball player makes a free throw with probability \(0.80\). If the player attempts six free throws, find the probability of making strictly between one and five shots.

Solution

\(X\) still denotes the number of successful free throws made by the player out of \(n=6\) attempts with the probability of success of each shot \(p=0.80\), and the probability of failure of each shot \(q=0.20\). That still gives us

\[X \sim \text{Bin}(6,0.80).\]

We are required to find

\[P(1<X<5)=P(X=2)+P(X=3)+P(X=4).\]

Using the binomial probability formula,

\[P(X=x)=\binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{\,n-x},\]

where \(n=6\) and \(p=0.80\),

\[P(X=2)=\binom{6}{2}(0.80)^2(0.20)^4=15(0.64)(0.0016)=0.01536,\] \[P(X=3)=\binom{6}{3}(0.80)^3(0.20)^3=20(0.512)(0.008)=0.08192,\]

and

\[P(X=4)=\binom{6}{4}(0.80)^4(0.20)^2=15(0.4096)(0.04)=0.24576.\]

Therefore,

\[P(1<X<5)=0.01536+0.08192+0.24576=0.34304.\]

Hence, the probability that the player makes strictly between one and five free throws is

\[P(1<X<5)=0.34304\approx0.3430.\]

If you want the endpoints included, it is clearer to state “between and including 1 and 5.”

Mean and Standard Deviation

Mean, Variance, and Standard Deviation of a Binomial Random Variable

For a binomial random variable \(X\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)\), the mean, variance, and standard deviation are

\[ \mu=np, \quad \sigma^2=npq, \quad \sigma=\sqrt{npq}.\]

These formulas eliminate the need to compute the mean and variance from the entire probability distribution.

Example 6

Suppose

\[X\sim\text{Bin}(6,0.80).\]

Find the mean, variance, and standard deviation.

Solution

The mean is

\[\mu=np=(6)(0.80)=4.80.\]

Note that the mean does not have to be a possible value of \(X\); it represents the long-term average outcome.

The variance is

\[\sigma^2=npq=(6)(0.80)(0.20)=0.960.\]

Therefore,

\[\sigma=\sqrt{0.96}\approx0.980.\]

Thus,

\[\mu=4.8,\qquad\sigma^2=0.96,\qquad\sigma\approx0.98.\]

Key Ideas

A binomial experiment has

  1. The experiment consists of a fixed number of trials, \(n\).
  2. Each trial has only two possible outcomes: success and failure.
  3. The trials are independent.
  4. The probability of success, \(p\), remains constant for every trial.

Always identify whether the problem asks for exactly, at most, at least, between, more than, or fewer than a specified number of successes before selecting the appropriate probability calculation.
