Chapter 9: Hypothesis Testing

Section 9.7: Summary

Hypothesis testing uses sample data to determine whether there is sufficient evidence to support a claim about a population parameter. The null hypothesis, \(H_0\), specifies the value or condition being tested and contains the equality component, while the alternative hypothesis, \(H_1\), represents the claim being tested. Tests may be two-tailed, left-tailed, or right-tailed depending on the alternative hypothesis.

A hypothesis test uses a significance level \(\alpha\) to control the probability of a Type I error, which occurs when a true null hypothesis is rejected. A Type II error occurs when a false null hypothesis is not rejected, with probability \(\beta\). The power of a test is \(\text{Power}=1-\beta\).

The general procedure is to state the hypotheses, select \(\alpha\), verify the conditions, calculate the test statistic, determine the critical value or \(p\)-value, make a decision, and state the conclusion in context. The decision is either to reject \(H_0\) or to fail to reject \(H_0\).

For a population mean with known standard deviation, use

\[z=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}.\]

When the population standard deviation is unknown, use Student's \(t\) distribution:

\[t=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}},\]

with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom. For a population proportion, use

\[z=\frac{\hat{p}-p_0}{\sqrt{p_0q_0/n}}.\]

The traditional method compares the test statistic with critical values, while the \(p\)-value method compares the \(p\)-value with \(\alpha\). Reject \(H_0\) when the test statistic falls in the rejection region or when \(p\leq\alpha\); otherwise, fail to reject \(H_0\). Increasing the sample size generally decreases sampling variability, reduces the probability of a Type II error, and increases the power of the test for a specified alternative effect.
